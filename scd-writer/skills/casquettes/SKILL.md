## Why the hat is a separate axis

The author writes on three surfaces, but the surfaces don't map one-to-one onto identities. The blog is always the personal dev hat. LinkedIn carries two hats, AI Manager at CEGAPE and founder of Isometria, and the same LinkedIn post format can be published under either. So the hat cannot be derived from the channel, nor the channel from the hat. (The three axes are in **writing-voice**.)

## The three hats

| | Personal dev | AI Manager, CEGAPE | Founder, Isometria |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surface** | Blog | LinkedIn (article + post) | LinkedIn (article + post) |
| **"I" is** | A developer learning in public | Someone accountable for AI work inside an organization | Someone building a company |
| **Mandate** | None. Writes to understand | Speaks near an employer, not for it | Speaks for a venture the author owns |
| **Can claim** | What was personally tried | What was observed in a professional context, at the abstraction the employer tolerates | What the company does and has shipped |
| **Cannot claim** | Institutional authority | Anything that reads as a CEGAPE position | Results the company hasn't produced |
| **Stake of error** | Reputation with peers | Employment, and the employer's | Commercial credibility |
| **Signature failure** | Sounding like a junior apologizing | Sounding like corporate comms | Sounding like a pitch |

## What changes, what never changes

**Never changes.** The six voice rules, the banned dash, the banned vocabulary, the forbidden rhetorical patterns, the anti-fabrication rule, the cardinal principle. A hat is not a licence to write worse. If a piece stops sounding like the author, the hat was used as an excuse.

**Changes.** The register (how far from the reader), the first person (`I` alone vs `I` inside a team vs `we` for the company), the level of detail permitted about real work, and the disclosure line that may be required.

The invariant is in writing-voice, section *Fixed voice, variable tone*. This skill only says how far the tone may travel.

A hat never suppresses a detector. If a hat seems to require a banned pattern, that is an arbitration and it goes through **faux-positifs**.

## Per-hat detail

### Personal dev, on the blog

The hat the blog was built for. First person singular, no institutional cover, no product to sell. Learning in public is the point: half-finished understanding is publishable if it is labelled as such, and "I got this wrong for two years" is the strongest material available.

Watch for: over-apologizing. Epistemic honesty is "I'm not certain the cause is X", not "sorry if this is obvious to everyone."

### AI Manager, CEGAPE, on LinkedIn

The riskiest hat, because the author writes *near* an employer without speaking *for* it. Everything drawn from professional work must be raised one level of abstraction: the pattern, never the client, the incident, the internal tool, or the number. Colleagues are not characters.

Watch for: corporate register creeping in: passive voice, nominalizations, "mettre en place", hedged conclusions nobody can disagree with. That register is exactly what marqueurs-lexicaux flags as register leveling, and it is the fastest way to sound generated.

### Founder, Isometria, on LinkedIn

The hat where the author is allowed to want something. Selling is legitimate here and does not need to be disguised as sharing: a disguised pitch reads worse than an open one.

Watch for: the pitch swallowing the substance. If the post would collapse without its final call to action, the substance was never there. That is slop-poli's definition, wearing a business suit.

## Choosing the hat

1. Blog → personal dev. No further question.
2. LinkedIn, and the material comes from the company the author owns → founder.
3. LinkedIn, and the material comes from employed work or from the field generally → AI Manager.
4. Genuinely ambiguous → **ask the author**. Never guess: guessing wrong on this axis is the one error with employment consequences.

A single piece wears one hat. If a draft needs two, it is two pieces.

## What the hats actually are

**CEGAPE** works for the French public sector: BPO, payroll and HRIS software, training. That fixes the abstraction ceiling of the AI Manager hat lower than most private-sector writing would need. A recognizable client is a recognizable public body, and the domain (payroll, HR records) is one where an anecdote about a bug is an anecdote about someone's salary. The pattern travels; the case never does.

**The AI Manager title is public**, since it is on the author's LinkedIn profile. Two consequences, and they pull in opposite directions. The hat may be worn openly: the author does not have to write around the role or pretend the vantage point is hypothetical. And every LinkedIn post is read by someone who can see the employer in one click, whether or not the post mentions it. There is no ambiguity to hide behind, which is why the abstraction rule above is not a precaution but the working constraint.

**Isometria** builds websites for small and mid-sized businesses. Digitalization and AI for that same audience are a direction, not a current offer. The founder hat may claim what has shipped for that audience and may say where the company is going, as long as the two are not phrased the same way. A roadmap written in the present tense is the pitch swallowing the substance.

**No disclosure is required.** The author writes in their own name, on every surface, under every hat. There is no "opinions are my own" formula to append, and none should be invented. But that absence is what makes the CEGAPE hat's abstraction rule load-bearing rather than decorative: nothing stands between the author and the employer except the author's own restraint.

**The blog is signed `sebc-dev`,** and links to CEGAPE and Isometria from the About page only, never from inside an article. The affiliations are discoverable, not asserted. A piece never needs to introduce them, and one that leans on the employer's name for authority is borrowing something the blog itself deliberately keeps at one remove.
